Why It Matters

Cash savings
aren't enough.

A dollar today buys less than a dollar did 10 years ago. Investing is how you stay ahead of inflation and build real wealth.

📉
What happens if you don't invest
−27%
$10,000 in a checking account (earning ~0.5%) over 10 years loses about 27% of its purchasing power after inflation. Your $10,000 feels like $7,300 in today's money.
📈
What happens when you invest
+$15,937
$10,000 invested in a broad index fund earning ~10% annually for 10 years becomes $25,937. That's the power of compound growth working for you instead of against you.
⏰
Time is your biggest asset
Someone who starts investing at 25 and stops at 35 will have more money at 65 than someone who starts at 35 and never stops — because of compound interest. Starting early beats investing more.
🧮
The rule of 72
Divide 72 by your annual return rate to find how many years it takes to double your money. At 10%: 72 ÷ 10 = 7.2 years to double. At 1% savings: 72 years. The math favors investing.
The Building Blocks

4 things you need
to understand first.

You don't need to master all of finance. These four concepts cover 90% of what most investors actually use.

Stocks

Pieces of a company

When you buy a stock, you own a small piece of that company. If the company grows and profits, your share is worth more. If it struggles, it's worth less. Higher potential reward, higher risk.

Risk level
Medium–High
Time horizon
5+ years
Avg return
~10%/yr
Bonds

Loans you give out

When you buy a bond, you're lending money to a government or company. They pay you back with interest over time. Lower potential returns, but much more stable than stocks. Good for near-term goals.

Risk level
Low–Medium
Time horizon
1–10 years
Avg return
~4–6%/yr
ETFs

Baskets of investments

An ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund) holds dozens or hundreds of stocks in one package. Instead of picking one company, you buy a basket. One share of an ETF = automatic diversification. This is where most beginners should start.

Risk level
Medium
Time horizon
5+ years
Avg return
Varies
Index Funds

The whole market at once

An index fund tracks a market index like the S&P 500 (the 500 largest US companies). You're not betting on one company — you're betting the US economy grows over time. Warren Buffett recommends them for most people.

Getting Started

Your first four steps.

You don't need a financial advisor or a lot of money to start. These four steps will get most people from zero to investing in under a week.

1

Build a $1,000 emergency fund first

Before you invest a dollar, make sure you have at least $1,000 in a savings account you can access immediately. Investing money you might need in 3 months will force you to sell at the wrong time. Aim for 3 months of expenses, then start investing.

💡 High-yield savings accounts (HYSA) earn 4–5% while you build this buffer
2

Open a tax-advantaged account

A 401(k) through your employer or a Roth IRA lets your investments grow tax-free (or tax-deferred). This is free money you're leaving on the table if you skip it. If your employer matches 401(k) contributions, contribute at least enough to get the full match — that's an immediate 50–100% return on those dollars.

💡 Roth IRA contribution limit: $7,000/year (2024). Anyone with earned income can open one.
3

Choose a simple brokerage and invest in index funds

Open an account with Fidelity, Vanguard, or Charles Schwab (all free). For most beginners: put everything into one or two broad index funds. Good starting options: VTI (total US stock market) or VOO (S&P 500). Don't try to pick individual stocks until you understand the basics.

💡 Fidelity and Schwab have no minimum to open. You can start with $1.
4

Automate and don't touch it

Set up automatic monthly deposits to your investment account. Pick an amount you won't miss — even $50/month matters. Then do not check it every day. The biggest investing mistake is panic-selling during a market dip. Time in the market beats timing the market, every time.

💡 Studies show investors who check their portfolios less often get better returns.
Common Pitfalls

Mistakes to avoid.

These are the errors that cost beginners thousands. Learning about them now is free.

✕

Waiting until you have "enough" money

Beginners often wait until they have $10,000+ to start. But time in the market is worth more than amount. Starting with $100 now beats starting with $10,000 in three years.

✓ Fix: Start with whatever you have. Increase over time.
✕

Panic-selling during a downturn

Markets drop 10–20% regularly. Every major crash in history has eventually recovered and gone higher. Selling when it drops locks in your loss. Holding through it means you eventually profit.

✓ Fix: Don't look at it. Automate. Think in decades.
✕

Trying to pick winning stocks

Even professional fund managers fail to beat the S&P 500 index consistently over 15+ years (roughly 90% of them). Individual stock picking is gambling for most people.

✓ Fix: Buy the index, not individual stocks.
✕

Ignoring fees

A 1% annual fee sounds small. On $100,000 over 20 years, it costs you over $30,000 in lost growth. ETFs like VOO charge 0.03%. Always check the expense ratio before buying.

✓ Fix: Choose funds with expense ratios under 0.2%.
✕

Chasing last year's hot investment

Crypto in 2021. AI stocks in 2023. Whatever went up 300% last year is already priced in. Chasing performance means buying high and usually selling low.

✓ Fix: Boring, consistent index fund investing wins long-term.
✕

Not using tax-advantaged accounts

Investing in a regular brokerage when you haven't maxed your Roth IRA is leaving free money on the table. A Roth IRA lets your gains grow completely tax-free. That can mean tens of thousands extra by retirement.

✓ Fix: Roth IRA first, then taxable brokerage.
Key Terms

The jargon, explained.

Every term you'll encounter when you start investing, in plain English.

401(k)
A retirement savings account offered by employers. Contributions are pre-tax, reducing your taxable income now. Many employers match contributions — always get the full match.
Roth IRA
An individual retirement account where you contribute after-tax money. All growth and withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. Best for people who expect to be in a higher tax bracket later.
Expense Ratio
The annual fee a fund charges, expressed as a percentage. A 0.03% expense ratio on $10,000 = $3/year. Keep this as low as possible — fees compound just like returns do.
Dividend
A portion of a company's profits paid regularly to shareholders. If you own shares of Apple and Apple pays a dividend, you get cash — just for holding the stock.
Diversification
Not putting all your eggs in one basket. Owning stocks across many companies, industries, and countries reduces the risk that one bad company wipes out your portfolio.
Bear Market
When the stock market drops 20% or more from its recent high. Historically, bear markets last ~9–16 months and are always followed by recovery. They're buying opportunities, not disasters.
Bull Market
When the stock market is rising and investor confidence is high — typically defined as a 20%+ gain from a recent low. The S&P 500 has spent most of its history in bull markets.
Dollar-Cost Averaging
Investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price. When prices are low, you automatically buy more shares. When prices are high, you buy fewer. Removes emotion from investing.
Compound Interest
Earning returns on your returns. $100 that earns 10% becomes $110. Next year, 10% on $110 = $121. Over decades, this snowball effect creates wealth that feels like magic.
Asset Allocation
How you split your portfolio between different asset types (stocks, bonds, cash). A common guideline: 110 minus your age = % in stocks. 30 years old → 80% stocks, 20% bonds.
Rebalancing
Periodically adjusting your portfolio back to your target allocation. If stocks surge and you're now 90% stocks when you wanted 80%, you sell some stocks and buy bonds to rebalance.
Capital Gains Tax
Tax you pay when you sell an investment for a profit. Short-term (held <1 year) = taxed as ordinary income. Long-term (held 1+ year) = 0–20% depending on income. This is why we buy and hold.
